## 3. What worked elsewhere (and why it might not work for you)
**Canva** — Australia, 2013-present · `[canva-2013]` · confidence: high
- Worked: Took design software that cost hundreds per seat and required expertise, and made the core task (drag, drop, template, export) work in a browser for non-designers. Freemium acquisition plus team/collaboration upsell. Built in Sydney, scaled globally online.
- Lesson: The biggest market is the people excluded by the incumbent's complexity. Simplify the workflow and give away the entry point.
- ⚠️ Survivorship bias: you are reading the winner. Thousands of near-identical attempts are not in any dataset.

**Atlassian** — Australia, 2002-present · `[atlassian-2002]` · confidence: high
- Worked: Founded in Sydney with ~$10k credit-card debt, no sales team for the first years, sold via download and later tried before buying. Built in a small market, so had to be export-ready from day one. Only took outside capital 8 years in.
- Lesson: Small home markets are a feature, not a bug: they force export-grade products. Product-led growth beats outbound sales for low-ACV tools.
- ⚠️ Survivorship bias: you are reading the winner. Thousands of near-identical attempts are not in any dataset.

**Afterpay / Buy Now Pay Later** — Australia, 2014-2022 · `[afterpay-2014]` · confidence: high
- Worked: Inverted the credit model: instead of charging the shopper interest, charged the merchant a fee for higher conversion and basket size. Removed the psychological pain of payment for a generation that distrusted credit cards. Acquired by Block for ~US$29B.
- Lesson: If your product makes the customer's life feel cheaper and the merchant's conversion better, growth is fast - but financial products eventually get regulated wherever they scale.
- ⚠️ Survivorship bias: you are reading the winner. Thousands of near-identical attempts are not in any dataset.

**Italian industrial districts (e.g. Sassuolo, Prato, Biella)** — Italy, 1950s-present · `[italian-industrial-districts]` · confidence: high
- Worked: Hundreds of tiny family firms each specialising in one step of a craft (ceramic tile, wool weaving, eyewear, packaging machinery) formed an ecosystem that could rival large factories while staying flexible and design-led. Shared local labour pools, rapid copying of innovations, deep craft skill transfer.
- Lesson: Decentralised clusters can beat vertical integration in design-led, high-mix manufacturing. But they need succession and brand investment to survive scale competition.

## 4. What killed the lookalikes
**Pets.com** — United States, 1998-2000 · `[pets-com-1998-2000]`
- Killed by: Shipping 20kg bags of dog food at negative margin, funded by Super Bowl ads, with no path to delivery density. The last-mile cost structure destroyed every incremental sale.
- Cultural factor: Dot-com era capital rewarded growth metrics and ignored gross margin; US market's cheap venture money delayed the reckoning

**Juicero** — United States, 2013-2017 · `[juicero-2013-2017]`
- Killed by: A $400 Wi-Fi juicer for $7-8 juice packs was beaten by the human hand - journalists squeezed the packs by hand and got the same result. The DRM-style QR gatekeeping removed the only remaining value.
- Cultural factor: Wellness-plus-VC culture funded a solution in search of a problem; Engineering elegance was mistaken for customer value

**Quibi** — United States, 2018-2020 · `[quibi-2018-2020]`
- Killed by: Solved a problem nobody had: paying for short-form video in a world of free TikTok and YouTube, mobile-only, no screenshots, no TV cast, no social sharing. Launch coincided with a pandemic that removed the commute use-case it was designed for.
- Cultural factor: Hollywood-figure pedigree was mistaken for market demand; Industry-insider consensus replaced user research

## 5. Model that fits your resources
- **Regulated-first market entry** (`[regulated-first]`) — In high-uncertainty-avoidance markets, certification, licensing and compliance are marketing assets, not costs.
  - Fits when: Target market scores high on uncertainty avoidance; Heavy product regulation (food, health, finance, children's products)
  - First test: List every licence/certification required and estimate the cost and time before building anything.
- **Localise the menu, keep the brand** (`[localise-menu-not-brand]`) — The brand can travel; the recipe, price point and packaging must be rebuilt per market.
  - Fits when: Food, beverage, retail or consumer packaged goods; Your home market is not your target market
  - First test: Take your top 3 products to 20 local consumers and ask which one they would replace an existing habit with. If none, rebuild the lineup.
- **Asset-light supply arbitrage** (`[asset-light-arbitrage]`) — Aggregate other people's assets or idle capacity instead of buying your own.
  - Fits when: Idle capacity exists and is easy to enumerate; Quality can be standardised through process rather than ownership
  - First test: Can you fulfil your first 20 orders without owning anything? If yes, stay asset-light until density forces otherwise.
- **Formalise what already exists informally** (`[formalise-informal]`) — Do not create new supply or new behaviour - organise existing informal supply (drivers, home cooks, tailors, tutors) and give it identity, payments and demand.
  - Fits when: Large informal sector in target market; Existing work happening off-books
  - First test: Recruit 20 existing informal providers and give them a simple booking channel. Do they bring their own customers?

## 9. Kill criteria
- You cannot name the specific person who will pay, or what they spend today on the alternative.
- Nobody pays anything (not even a deposit) in 7 days after seeing a real offer.
- Your true cost per unit is above what the market already pays for the alternative, with no path to close the gap in 3 months.
- The purchase requires behaviour change bigger than 'swap brand' — i.e. the customer must learn something new, buy new hardware, or change a habit tied to family or religion.
